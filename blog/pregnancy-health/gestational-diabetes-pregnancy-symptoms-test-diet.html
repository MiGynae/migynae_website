Gestational Diabetes During Pregnancy: Symptoms, Testing and Food Guide

Medically reviewed by Dr. Shuchita Batra, MBBS, MS (Obstetrics & Gynaecology)

Being told you have high blood sugar during pregnancy can be worrying. You may immediately wonder whether your baby is safe, whether you will need insulin injections and, most importantly, what you can eat now!

First, take a breath. Gestational diabetes (GDM) is quite common during pregnancy and can usually be managed with the right diet, activity and medical care. It is not your fault, and it does not mean you have to give up all your favourite foods.

Let's understand why it happens, when you need testing and how you can manage it while still enjoying a balanced Indian diet.

Understanding Gestational Diabetes: Why It Happens During Pregnancy

Gestational diabetes is a condition in which blood sugar levels become higher than normal during pregnancy.

During pregnancy, your body produces hormones that make it harder for insulin to work effectively. Insulin is the hormone that helps move sugar from your blood into your body's cells, where it is used for energy.

Normally, your pancreas produces extra insulin to manage this change. But sometimes, it cannot produce enough to meet the increased demand. As a result, sugar starts building up in your blood.

This is called gestational diabetes.

One important thing to remember: GDM is not simply caused by eating too much sugar. Pregnancy-related hormonal changes play a major role, and even women who eat healthy and stay active can develop it.

Symptoms of Gestational Diabetes: Why You May Not Notice Anything

Most women with gestational diabetes do not experience any noticeable symptoms. You may feel perfectly normal even when your blood sugar levels are high.

Some women may notice:

  • Increased thirst.
  • Passing urine more frequently.
  • Unusual tiredness.
  • Blurred vision.
  • Recurrent urinary or vaginal infections.

But these symptoms can also occur in an otherwise normal pregnancy.

That's why you cannot identify gestational diabetes just by how you feel. A blood sugar screening test is the only reliable way to detect it.

Risk Factors: Who Is More Likely to Develop GDM?

Gestational diabetes can happen to any pregnant woman, but certain factors may increase your chances.

You may have a higher risk if you:

  • Are overweight or obese.
  • Have a family history of diabetes, especially in parents or siblings.
  • Have PCOS.
  • Had gestational diabetes in an earlier pregnancy.
  • Previously delivered a baby with a high birth weight.
  • Have a history of prediabetes or elevated blood sugar.

However, having none of these risk factors does not mean you cannot develop GDM.

Since gestational diabetes is relatively common in India, screening is recommended broadly rather than only for women considered high-risk.

GTT During Pregnancy: When and How the Test Is Done

The Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT), also called the Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT), checks how your body handles sugar.

In India, many clinics follow the DIPSI method, which is convenient because it does not require fasting.

Your doctor may recommend testing:

  • At your first antenatal visit.
  • Again around 24–28 weeks if your initial test is normal.
  • Later in pregnancy if you have additional risk factors or a medical reason for repeat testing.

Don't miss the test just because you feel healthy. GDM often develops without symptoms.

DIPSI Test in India: Do You Need to Fast?

DIPSI stands for Diabetes in Pregnancy Study Group of India. It is a commonly used testing method in Indian antenatal care.

Here's how it works:

  • You are given a drink containing 75 grams of glucose.
  • You do not need to fast before drinking it.
  • Your blood sample is taken two hours after consuming the glucose drink.

The blood sugar result is used to assess whether you have gestational diabetes. Under the DIPSI criteria, a two-hour blood glucose value of 140 mg/dL or higher is used to diagnose GDM.

Some hospitals may follow a different OGTT protocol, such as a fasting test with additional blood samples. So always confirm whether your test requires fasting and follow the instructions given by your hospital.

Understanding Your GTT Report: What Do the Numbers Mean?

You may come across different blood sugar cutoffs when searching online. That's because different testing methods use different criteria.

For example, DIPSI uses the two-hour value after a 75-gram glucose drink, whereas other protocols measure fasting, one-hour and two-hour blood sugar levels.

This is why you should not compare your result with a random number found online.

Ask your doctor:

  • Which testing method was used?
  • Is my result normal or above the recommended range?
  • Do I need repeat testing or further monitoring?

Your doctor will interpret the result using the correct criteria for your test and decide what needs to be done next.

Diagnosed with Gestational Diabetes: What Happens Next?

First, don't panic. A diagnosis of GDM does not automatically mean you will need insulin or a Caesarean delivery.

Your doctor will usually recommend a few changes to help bring your blood sugar levels under control. These may include:

  • A personalised meal plan.
  • Regular blood sugar monitoring.
  • Walking or other suitable physical activity.
  • More frequent antenatal check-ups.
  • Medication, if needed.

Many women achieve good sugar control with nutrition and physical activity alone. If your readings remain above your doctor's targets, medicines may be added.

The aim is to keep your blood sugar within the recommended range throughout pregnancy.

Indian Diet for Gestational Diabetes: What Should You Eat?

You do not need to stop eating carbohydrates completely. Your body and your growing baby still need adequate nutrition.

The key is to choose better carbohydrate sources, watch your portions and combine them with protein and fibre. A few simple guidelines can help:

  • Eat regular meals: Avoid skipping meals or going for long periods without eating.
  • Add protein: Include dal, chana, rajma, paneer, curd, tofu, eggs or other suitable protein sources.
  • Choose fibre-rich foods: Include vegetables, whole fruits, oats, whole grains and pulses.
  • Watch your portions: Even healthy foods can raise blood sugar if eaten in excessive quantities.
  • Avoid sugary drinks: Replace packaged juices, soft drinks and sweetened beverages with water, unsweetened chaas or other suitable drinks.
  • Spread your carbohydrates: Divide your food across three meals and two or three small snacks, as advised by your dietitian.

A simple way to plan your plate is to fill half with non-starchy vegetables, one quarter with protein-rich foods and the remaining quarter with roti, rice or another carbohydrate source.

Your exact portions should be adjusted according to your nutritional needs and blood sugar readings.

Rice and Roti in Gestational Diabetes: What Can You Eat?

This is probably one of the biggest worries after a GDM diagnosis! The good news is that you do not have to completely stop eating rice or roti.

You can include both in your diet, but portion size and how you combine them matter. Some practical choices:

  • Choose whole-wheat roti or try millets such as jowar, bajra and ragi.
  • Include measured portions of rice rather than large servings.
  • Combine roti or rice with dal, vegetables, curd, paneer or tofu.
  • Avoid eating a large portion of rice or roti without protein and vegetables.
  • Check your post-meal sugar readings to understand how your body responds.

Remember, switching to brown rice or millets does not automatically make a meal suitable for GDM. Portion size and your individual blood sugar response matter too.

Fruits and Snacks: Making Better Everyday Choices

You don't have to stop eating fruits just because you have gestational diabetes. Choose whole fruits rather than fruit juices, and pay attention to portion sizes.

Some convenient snack ideas include:

  • A small serving of guava, apple, pear or orange.
  • Roasted chana.
  • A handful of unsalted nuts.
  • Plain curd.
  • Vegetable sticks with hummus.
  • A small portion of sprouts, cooked safely.

Try pairing fruit with a protein or healthy-fat source when suitable. Your dietitian can help decide the right portions and timing. Avoid replacing whole fruits with packaged fruit juices, which can deliver a large amount of sugar without the same fibre.

Festivals, Sweets and Gestational Diabetes

Having GDM does not mean you have to feel left out at every family celebration. However, sweets and desserts can raise your blood sugar quickly, so portion control is important.

A few practical tips:

  • Avoid eating sweets on an empty stomach.
  • If your doctor or dietitian permits, enjoy a small portion occasionally.
  • Avoid combining several sweet dishes in one meal.
  • Keep your regular meals balanced.
  • Check your blood sugar as advised.

You may need to adjust your meal plan around celebrations, so discuss this with your dietitian rather than making changes to your medicines or skipping meals.

Insulin During Pregnancy: Is It Necessary and Is It Safe?

Not every woman with gestational diabetes needs insulin. Your doctor may first recommend dietary changes, physical activity and regular sugar monitoring. If your blood sugar remains above the target range despite these measures, medication may be needed.

Insulin is a well-established and commonly preferred treatment for gestational diabetes when medication is required. It does not cross the placenta in significant amounts.

In some situations, your doctor may also discuss metformin after considering its benefits and limitations.

Needing insulin does not mean you failed to control your sugar through diet. Pregnancy hormones can increase insulin resistance, and sometimes your body simply needs additional support. Never start, stop or adjust diabetes medication without your doctor's advice.

How Gestational Diabetes Can Affect Your Baby and Pregnancy

When blood sugar remains high during pregnancy, it can increase the risk of certain complications. These may include:

  • Your baby growing larger than expected.
  • Increased chances of difficult vaginal delivery or birth injuries.
  • High blood pressure and pre-eclampsia in the mother.
  • Low blood sugar in the newborn after birth.
  • The baby needing additional observation after delivery.

The good news is that timely diagnosis, regular monitoring and proper treatment can significantly reduce these risks. Your doctor may recommend additional growth scans or fetal monitoring depending on your blood sugar control and other pregnancy-related factors.

After Delivery: Does Gestational Diabetes Go Away?

For most women, blood sugar levels return to normal after childbirth because the pregnancy-related hormonal changes begin to settle.

However, having gestational diabetes means you have a higher chance of developing type 2 diabetes in the future. That's why follow-up testing is important even if you feel completely fine after childbirth.

Your doctor will usually recommend:

  • A postpartum blood sugar test, commonly around 6–12 weeks after delivery.
  • Further diabetes screening at regular intervals.
  • Healthy eating and physical activity.
  • Blood sugar testing before planning your next pregnancy.

Do not skip your postpartum test. It is an important part of looking after your long-term health.

A final reminder: Gestational diabetes can feel overwhelming at first, especially when you start worrying about every meal. But you don't have to figure it all out alone. With the right diet, regular monitoring and guidance from your doctor, you can manage your blood sugar and have a healthy pregnancy.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Can gestational diabetes be controlled with diet alone?

Yes. Many women manage gestational diabetes with a personalised diet plan, regular activity and blood sugar monitoring. If readings remain high, your doctor may recommend medication.

2. Can I eat rice and roti if I have gestational diabetes?

Yes. You can include both in controlled portions. Combine them with protein-rich foods and vegetables, and follow the portions recommended by your dietitian.

3. Does the DIPSI test require fasting?

No. The DIPSI test uses a 75-gram glucose drink, and you do not need to fast beforehand. Your blood sugar is checked two hours later. Other OGTT methods may require fasting.

4. Can I eat fruits if I have gestational diabetes?

Yes. Choose whole fruits in suitable portions rather than fruit juices. Your dietitian can help you decide which fruits and quantities fit your meal plan.

5. Will I definitely need insulin injections?

No. Insulin is prescribed when lifestyle changes alone are not enough to achieve your blood sugar targets. Needing insulin is not your fault, and it is an established treatment during pregnancy.

6. Does gestational diabetes go away after delivery?

In most women, blood sugar levels return to normal after childbirth. However, follow-up testing is essential because the risk of developing type 2 diabetes later in life is higher.

7. When should I get my blood sugar tested after delivery?

A postpartum test is generally recommended around 6–12 weeks after delivery. Your doctor will advise the appropriate test and further screening schedule.
