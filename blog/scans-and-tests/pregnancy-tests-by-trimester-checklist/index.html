Pregnancy Tests by Trimester: A Complete Checklist for Expecting Mothers

Medically reviewed by Dr. Shuchita Batra, MBBS, MS (Obstetrics & Gynaecology)

Once your pregnancy is confirmed, your doctor will recommend a series of blood tests, urine tests and scans at different stages. You may wonder why so many tests are needed when you are feeling perfectly fine!

The reason is simple: different tests help check different aspects of your health and your baby's development. Some are done only once, while others need to be repeated as pregnancy progresses.

To make things easier, here's a trimester-wise checklist of the important pregnancy tests commonly recommended in India, what they check and when you may need them.

First Trimester Tests (Weeks 1–12): Your Initial Pregnancy Check-up

Your first antenatal visit is ideally scheduled before 12 weeks. This is when your doctor gets a complete picture of your health and checks for conditions that may need attention during pregnancy.

1. Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Haemoglobin

This blood test checks your haemoglobin, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.

It helps identify:

  • Anaemia (low haemoglobin).
  • Low platelet counts.
  • Other abnormalities in your blood count that may need further evaluation.

Anaemia is common during pregnancy, so checking your haemoglobin early helps your doctor decide whether you need iron supplementation or further tests.

2. Blood Group, Rh Factor and Antibody Screening

Your blood group (A, B, AB or O) and Rh status (positive or negative) are checked early in pregnancy.

If you are Rh-negative, your doctor may also check for antibodies that could affect your baby's red blood cells.

Knowing your blood group early helps your doctor plan the right precautions and treatment if needed.

3. Infection Screening: HIV, Hepatitis B and Syphilis

These tests screen for infections that can sometimes pass from mother to baby during pregnancy or childbirth.

Early detection is important because appropriate treatment and precautions can greatly reduce the risk of transmission.

Your doctor may also recommend screening for other infections depending on your medical history and local guidelines.

4. Thyroid Test (TSH)

Thyroid hormones play an important role in your baby's growth and development, especially during early pregnancy.

A TSH blood test helps check whether your thyroid is functioning properly.

If you already have thyroid problems or your result is abnormal, your doctor may recommend additional thyroid tests and repeat monitoring during pregnancy.

5. Blood Sugar Test (DIPSI)

In India, the DIPSI test is commonly used to screen for gestational diabetes.

You are given a drink containing 75 grams of glucose, and your blood sugar is checked two hours later. Unlike some other glucose tolerance tests, the DIPSI method does not require fasting.

Even if you have no symptoms or family history of diabetes, your doctor may recommend this test at your first antenatal visit.

If the result is normal, it is generally repeated after 24 weeks as advised by your doctor.

6. Urine Routine Examination and Urine Culture

A urine test helps check for:

  • Protein, which may need further assessment.
  • Sugar in the urine.
  • Pus cells or other signs of infection.

Your doctor may also recommend a urine culture to detect bacteria, even when you have no burning or urinary symptoms. This is important because an untreated urinary infection during pregnancy can sometimes cause complications.

7. Additional Screening for Thalassemia and Other Blood Disorders

Depending on your family history, blood count and regional risk, your doctor may recommend screening for thalassemia or sickle cell disease.

These tests help identify inherited blood conditions that may be passed on to the baby. If you are found to be a carrier, your partner may also need testing.

8. NT Scan and Double Marker Test

Usually between 11 weeks and 13 weeks 6 days

These are first-trimester screening tests used to estimate the risk of certain chromosomal conditions, including Down syndrome.

The NT scan measures a small fluid-filled space at the back of your baby's neck. The Double Marker is a blood test that measures two pregnancy-related proteins.

Together with other factors, these results help calculate your baby's screening risk.

Remember, these are screening tests, not diagnostic tests. A higher-risk result does not mean your baby definitely has a chromosomal condition.

For a detailed explanation, read our guide on Pregnancy Scans in India.

Second Trimester Tests (Weeks 13–26): Checking Your Baby's Development

The second trimester is an important time for checking your baby's organ development and screening for conditions that may develop as pregnancy progresses.

1. Quadruple Marker Test (If First-Trimester Screening Was Missed)

Usually between 14 and 20 weeks

If you missed the NT and Double Marker screening window, your doctor may offer a Quadruple Marker blood test.

It measures four substances in your blood to estimate the risk of certain chromosomal conditions and neural tube defects.

It is important to understand that this is also a screening test, not a diagnosis.

2. Anomaly Scan (Level II or TIFFA)

Usually between 18 and 22 weeks

This is a detailed ultrasound that checks your baby's developing organs and body structures, including the brain, heart, spine, kidneys, limbs and other important structures.

It also helps assess the placenta and amniotic fluid.

This scan is one of the most important pregnancy milestones, so make sure you book it within the recommended window.

3. Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT)

Usually repeated after 24 weeks if earlier screening was normal

Your doctor may repeat the glucose test to check for gestational diabetes, even if your earlier result was normal.

Pregnancy hormones can increase insulin resistance as pregnancy progresses, which is why a repeat test is important.

The method may be DIPSI or another glucose tolerance protocol, depending on your hospital.

4. Repeat Haemoglobin Test

Your doctor may repeat your haemoglobin or complete blood count around 24–28 weeks to check for anaemia.

Your baby's growth and the increase in your blood volume can make anaemia more likely as pregnancy progresses.

If your haemoglobin is low, your doctor may adjust your iron treatment or recommend additional investigations.

5. Repeat Urine Examination

Urine testing continues during antenatal visits to check for protein and other abnormalities.

A repeat urine examination or culture may also be recommended around this stage, particularly if you have a history of urinary infections or an earlier abnormal result.

6. Rh Antibody Screening and Anti-D Planning

If you have an Rh-negative blood group, your doctor may repeat your antibody screening around 28 weeks.

If you are Rh-negative and have not already developed anti-D antibodies, your doctor will discuss Anti-D injection, commonly given around 28 weeks, to prevent Rh sensitisation.

The injection is also considered after certain bleeding episodes or procedures, depending on the stage of pregnancy.

Third Trimester Tests (Weeks 27–40): Preparing for Delivery

As you enter the final months, the focus shifts towards your baby's growth, your health and preparing for childbirth.

1. Repeat Haemoglobin Test

A further haemoglobin check may be recommended around 36–40 weeks to make sure your levels are adequate before delivery.

This is particularly important if you had anaemia earlier in pregnancy or needed iron treatment.

2. Blood Pressure and Urine Protein Checks

At your antenatal visits, your doctor will continue checking your blood pressure and urine for protein.

These checks help identify conditions such as pregnancy-induced hypertension and pre-eclampsia, which may develop even if your earlier readings were normal.

Do not skip these routine checks just because you are feeling well.

3. Growth Scan and Doppler (If Advised)

Your doctor may recommend a third-trimester ultrasound to check:

  • Your baby's estimated weight and growth.
  • Amniotic fluid levels.
  • Placental position.
  • Your baby's position.

A Doppler scan may also be recommended if there are concerns about fetal growth or placental blood flow.

Not every woman needs a growth scan or Doppler at the same time. Your doctor will decide based on your pregnancy and previous findings.

4. Repeat Urine Tests or Other Investigations

If you have urinary symptoms, previous abnormal urine reports, high blood pressure or other medical concerns, your doctor may recommend additional urine or blood tests.

These are planned according to your individual needs rather than being compulsory for every pregnancy.

5. Group B Streptococcus (GBS) Screening, Where Recommended

Some hospitals and clinical protocols recommend a vaginal and rectal swab around 35–37 weeks to check for Group B Streptococcus (GBS).

GBS is a bacterium that can be present without causing symptoms but may occasionally cause infection in a newborn during delivery.

Screening and prevention practices vary between countries and hospitals in India. Ask your doctor whether this test is part of your hospital's protocol.

Anti-D Injection During Pregnancy: Who Needs It?

If your blood group is Rh-negative, Anti-D is an important part of pregnancy care when you have not already developed anti-D antibodies.

Your doctor will generally:

  • Check your blood group and Rh status early in pregnancy.
  • Screen for Rh antibodies.
  • Recommend routine Anti-D prophylaxis around 28 weeks when indicated.
  • Advise an additional dose after certain sensitising events, such as bleeding or an invasive procedure.
  • Check your baby's blood group after delivery and advise postpartum Anti-D if required.

Anti-D helps prevent your body from developing antibodies that could affect a future Rh-positive pregnancy.

If you are Rh-negative, make sure your gynaecologist knows about it from your first visit.

How Often Do You Need Antenatal Check-ups?

Pregnancy tests are only one part of antenatal care. Regular check-ups are equally important because some conditions can develop between scheduled tests.

For an uncomplicated pregnancy, a commonly recommended schedule is:

  • Up to 28 weeks: Approximately once a month.
  • 28–36 weeks: Every two weeks.
  • After 36 weeks: Usually once a week until delivery.

At these visits, your doctor may check your blood pressure, weight, urine protein, baby's growth and heartbeat, and discuss any symptoms or concerns.

High-risk pregnancies may need more frequent appointments and additional investigations.

Free Pregnancy Tests and Check-ups Under Government Schemes in India

If you are receiving antenatal care at a government health facility, you may be eligible for free investigations and ultrasound services.

Under the Pradhan Mantri Surakshit Matritva Abhiyaan (PMSMA), free antenatal check-ups are provided at designated government health facilities on the 9th of every month, particularly for women in their second and third trimesters.

The service package may include:

  • Doctor or specialist consultation.
  • Essential blood and urine investigations.
  • Ultrasound services.
  • Pregnancy risk assessment and counselling.

The exact services available can vary by facility. Contact your nearest government health centre to find out where and how to access them.

What If a Pregnancy Test Report Is Abnormal?

Seeing a result marked 'high', 'low' or 'abnormal' can be frightening, especially when you are pregnant.

But remember, an abnormal result does not always mean something is seriously wrong.

Sometimes your doctor may simply recommend:

  • Repeating the test.
  • Doing a more specific investigation.
  • Getting a specialist opinion.
  • Monitoring the result more frequently.

Don't compare your report with someone else's or try to interpret every value on your own. The meaning of a result depends on your pregnancy week, medical history and other findings.

Always review your reports with your gynaecologist.

A little reminder: Keep a folder of all your blood reports, urine tests and ultrasound scans. Carry it to every antenatal appointment so your doctor can follow your pregnancy journey easily.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What are the most important blood tests in the first trimester?

Common first-visit tests include CBC or haemoglobin, blood group and Rh factor, HIV, Hepatitis B, syphilis, thyroid (TSH), blood sugar screening and urine examination. Additional tests may be advised depending on your history.

2. Do all pregnancy blood tests require fasting?

No. Tests such as CBC, blood group, HIV and thyroid testing generally do not require fasting. The DIPSI glucose test also does not require fasting, but other glucose tolerance protocols may. Always confirm with your laboratory.

3. When is the GTT test done during pregnancy?

GDM screening is offered at the first antenatal visit under FOGSI guidance and repeated after 24 weeks if the initial result is normal. The exact timing and method may vary according to your doctor's protocol.

4. Can a CBC test confirm pregnancy?

No. A CBC checks your blood count and helps detect anaemia and other abnormalities. Pregnancy is confirmed through an hCG test in urine or blood.

5. What happens if I miss my NT scan?

If you miss the NT screening window, speak to your gynaecologist about other available screening options, such as the Quadruple Marker test or NIPT, depending on your pregnancy week and circumstances.

6. Are pregnancy tests free in India?

Eligible antenatal investigations and ultrasound services may be available free at designated government facilities under PMSMA. Ask your nearest government health centre about the services offered.

7. Do I need to repeat my blood tests in every trimester?

Some tests, such as haemoglobin screening and urine checks, may be repeated during pregnancy. Others, such as blood group testing, are usually done at the first visit, with antibody screening repeated when indicated. Your doctor will decide the repeat tests based on your reports and medical history.

Your pregnancy checklist is not meant to overwhelm you. Each test has a purpose, and knowing what to expect can make your antenatal visits much easier. Keep your appointments, save your reports and ask your doctor whenever something is unclear. A well-informed mother is better prepared for every stage of pregnancy.
