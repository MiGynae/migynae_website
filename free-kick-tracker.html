When do babies start kicking, and when should I start counting kicks?

Most women first feel movement somewhere between 18 and 24 weeks — earlier if it's not your first pregnancy, since you already know what to look for. Feeling movement is different from formal kick counting, though: most doctors suggest starting deliberate, daily kick counts from around 28 weeks, once movement is frequent and established enough to track a reliable pattern.

How many kicks should I feel, and how often is that normal?

There are two well-established but slightly different clinical approaches here, and it's worth knowing both rather than picking one blindly. One common benchmark: feeling 10 movements — kicks, flutters, rolls, anything — within about 2 hours is considered reassuring. The other approach, used by the NHS in the UK, deliberately avoids a fixed number altogether: the guidance there is that there's no universal "normal count," and what matters far more is learning your own baby's usual pattern and noticing any real change from it, at any time of day.

Which one should you use?
Both are legitimate — ask your own doctor which approach they'd like you to follow, and use MiGynae's tracker to log against whichever method they recommend. What matters most isn't the exact number, it's building a clear sense of your baby's normal pattern early, so a real change is obvious later.

How do I actually count kicks — a step-by-step method?

A simple, widely used method: pick a time your baby is normally active (often after a meal), sit or lie on your left side to help blood flow, and time how long it takes to feel 10 movements — count kicks, flutters, rolls, and swishes, not just hard kicks. Do this around the same time each day so you're comparing like with like.

What counts as a "kick"? Do flutters, hiccups, and rolls count too?

Yes — count anything you can distinctly feel: kicks, flutters, rolls, stretches, and swishes. Rhythmic, repetitive twitching is often hiccups rather than a kick; it's fine to note it, but it's a different sensation and some trackers separate it out, since hiccups don't reflect the same kind of active movement.

Why does my baby kick more at night or after I eat?

Babies have their own sleep-wake cycles in the womb, and many are more active in the evening. A rise in your blood sugar after eating, and simply sitting still enough to notice movement you'd otherwise miss while busy during the day, both contribute to that "more active at night" feeling too.

How can I get my baby to move if I'm trying to do a count?

Having a cold drink, eating something sweet, and lying down on your left side in a quiet spot are the most commonly suggested ways to encourage movement for a count — babies often respond within 10–20 minutes. If nothing happens after trying this, that's exactly the situation covered in our companion guide on reduced movement.

Do kicks get weaker or less frequent near the due date because there's less room?

This is a common but incorrect assumption. Movement should not meaningfully decrease as you approach your due date — if anything, a real, noticeable drop late in pregnancy is one of the more important signals to act on, not dismiss as "running out of room." We cover exactly what to do about that in the companion article below.

Is it normal for kicks to hurt or feel like they're in my ribs?

Yes, this is common, especially in the third trimester as your baby grows and has less room to move — sharp jabs under the ribs are a frequent complaint and not usually a concern on their own. Shifting position or gently pressing back where you feel it often helps.

How accurate is a kick counter app — can it replace a doctor visit or NST?

A kick counter is an awareness and pattern-tracking tool, not a diagnostic device — it can't replace a non-stress test (NST) or a doctor's evaluation. Its real value is helping you notice, early and clearly, when something is different from your baby's usual pattern, so you know when it's time to get that professional evaluation rather than waiting and wondering.

Is kick counting necessary for every pregnancy, or only high-risk ones?

It's specifically recommended for higher-risk pregnancies, but it's genuinely useful for any pregnancy — it costs nothing, takes a few minutes a day, and builds exactly the kind of awareness that helps you act quickly if something does change.

How do I record kick counts so I can show my doctor a pattern?

Start logging the date and time in the kick tracker, roughly what time you counted, and how long it took to reach your target count. A few weeks of this turns "I think movement has been normal" into an actual pattern you can describe precisely — genuinely useful at a checkup, and essential if you ever need to explain a change.

I have an anterior placenta and can't feel kicks clearly — what should I do?

An anterior placenta (at the front of the uterus) can cushion and muffle movement, so it's common to feel kicks later and less distinctly. Keep tracking anyway using whatever you can feel as your own baseline — your "normal" will just look a little different, and that's fine as long as you know your own pattern.

Twin pregnancy — how do I count kicks for two babies?

It's genuinely harder to attribute movement to a specific twin, and most doctors don't expect you to separate them precisely. The practical approach is to track total movement and overall activity level rather than trying to count each baby individually — and to mention the twin pregnancy specifically if you ever need to discuss reduced movement with your doctor, since the guidance can differ slightly.
