Pain Relief in Labour

The Epidural, explained

The most effective pain relief in labour, and one of the most misunderstood. Here is what it actually is, so you can decide with confidence.

An epidural can make labour far more comfortable while you stay awake and in control. Knowing how it works takes the fear out of the decision.
1

What an epidural is

A pain-relief injection given in your lower back during labour. It numbs the lower half of your body, so contractions hurt far less, while you stay fully awake and alert. A thin, soft tube is left in place so the medicine can be topped up as your labour goes on.

When can you ask?

When labour pain becomes hard to manage. It is usually given once labour is well established, but the timing is decided with your doctor and anaesthetist based on how you are progressing. There is no prize for suffering — it is okay to ask.

2

What to expect

1
Getting it placedYou will sit or lie curled forward and stay as still as you can for a few minutes.
2
A gentle startA small local injection numbs the skin first, so placing the tube feels like pressure, not sharp pain.
3
Relief beginsThe pain usually eases within about 10 to 20 minutes.
4
You will be monitoredYour blood pressure and your baby's heartbeat are watched closely. You may need a small tube to empty your bladder.
5
Heavy legsYour legs will feel heavy or numb. That is normal and wears off after birth.
3

Myths vs facts

MythIt causes lifelong back pain.
FactResearch does not support this. Some soreness at the spot for a day or two can happen.
MythIt always leads to a C-section.
FactIt does not. It may make pushing a little longer, and sometimes a little help is needed to birth the baby.
MythYou cannot push with an epidural.
FactYou can. Your team guides you on when to push, and many women still feel pressure.
Good to know

Most women do very well. Some feel shivering, itching, or a short drop in blood pressure that the team manages quickly. A bad headache afterwards is uncommon, and serious problems are rare. A few conditions — like certain bleeding problems, some spine issues, or blood-thinning medicines — can make an epidural unsuitable, so your anaesthetist will always check first.

4

Ask your doctor or anaesthetist

A few good questions to feel ready:

  1. Am I a good candidate for an epidural?
  2. When can I have it, and is it ever too late?
  3. Will I still be able to move or push?
  4. What side effects should I watch for?
